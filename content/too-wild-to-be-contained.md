## Intro

An interactive storytelling website about what happened to Wild & Lethal Trash, Walter Van Beirendonck’s most iconic brand. It launched in 1993 and turned out too wild for the market that tried to contain it.

## Body

Visitors release the W&LT mascot to start the story, then wake, drag and scroll their way through the label’s rise, its collaboration with Mustang and the breaking point where radical expression collided with large-scale fashion commerce. Built with GSAP, ScrollTrigger, Draggable and Lottie.

## Step 1: The story

Research narrowed down to one focus: the end of W&LT. I split its history into chapters, and those became the structure of the site: too wild, too big, the container, the breaking point and the exit.

---

### W&LT was too wild to be contained

Wild & Lethal Trash (W&LT), founded in 1993 by Belgian designer Walter Van Beirendonck (Antwerp Six), was less a conventional fashion label and more an experimental platform. It used fashion to explore power, fear, sexuality, violence and identity rather than seasonal trends. W&LT was the most radical expression of Van Beirendonck’s ideas, though its extremity eventually clashed with commercial demands.

### Too wild: radical content and visual confrontation (1993-1998)

W&LT deliberately positioned itself outside the dominant fashion logic of the 1990s. At a time when European fashion increasingly moved toward minimalism (notably designers such as Helmut Lang, Jil Sander and later Calvin Klein), W&LT embraced maximalism, excess and provocation using fluorescent colour palettes, cartoon-like graphics and recurring characters, aggressive typography and direct textual slogans printed on garments.

- W&LT Autumn/Winter 1995-96: featured explicit slogans and imagery dealing with fear, aggression and social anxiety, challenging the boundaries of runway acceptability.
- W&LT Spring/Summer 1997: intensified the use of cartoon violence and political symbolism, blurring the line between fashion, street culture and protest imagery.
- W&LT Autumn/Winter 1998: further escalated confrontational graphics and messages, at a moment when retailers were increasingly resistant to “unwearable” or politically charged fashion.

Rather than offering ambiguity, W&LT’s designs were deliberately direct and often uncomfortable. This refusal to soften or aestheticise its message became central to the label’s identity, but also increasingly problematic within commercial contexts.

### Too big: scaling up and structural tension (1996-1999)

By the mid-to-late 1990s, W&LT had gained substantial international visibility, particularly among youth culture, club scenes and alternative fashion markets. This success led to expanded production volumes and global distribution, pushing the label beyond its original small-scale, flexible structure. Key structural issues included:

- Outsourcing of production, reducing Van Beirendonck’s direct control over materials, colour accuracy and finishing.
- Difficulty reproducing complex prints and unconventional fabrics consistently at scale.
- Increased pressure to meet deadlines, cost targets and retail expectations.

What had functioned effectively as an experimental, concept-driven operation became constrained by the rigidity of larger production and distribution systems.

### The container: Mustang and commercial constraint (circa 1997-2000)

A major turning point came with the collaboration with Mustang, a German denim company that produced and distributed parts of the W&LT line in the late 1990s. Key points of tension:

- Mustang required denim-based continuity, recognisable silhouettes and repeatable graphic elements.
- W&LT’s creative logic depended on constant reinvention, escalation and unpredictability.
- Retailers increasingly rejected designs described as “too extreme”, “too political” and “too difficult to sell”.

In a market increasingly driven by efficiency, recognisability and sell-through, W&LT’s refusal to stabilise its visual language became a liability. The collaboration effectively acted as a container: a structure that made visible the incompatibility between radical expression and large-scale fashion commerce.

### The breaking point: irreconcilable values (circa 2000-2001)

By the turn of the millennium, creative freedom within W&LT had become conditional. Design decisions were no longer driven solely by conceptual urgency, but by questions of feasibility and marketability. The core conflict crystallised into a simple but fundamental question: what needs to be said versus what can be sold.

### The exit: preserving integrity through closure

W&LT ceased operations in the early 2000s (around 2001-2002). The decision was not the result of declining relevance or creative exhaustion, but a conscious refusal to compromise. Ending the label allowed W&LT to remain a closed, coherent body of work rather than a diluted brand surviving through adaptation.

### Uncontained: legacy and influence

Today, W&LT exists through archives, museum exhibitions and academic discourse. Its influence is evident in:

- Contemporary streetwear
- Graphic-heavy fashion labels
- The continued use of text, symbolism and clothing as political communication

W&LT’s significance lies not in longevity or commercial success, but in its demonstration of where the fashion system draws its limits. In this sense, W&LT was not a failure of fashion, but a revelation of its boundaries. W&LT was too wild to be contained.

## Step 2: Wireframes

Every chapter got a huge typographic statement and one interaction with the puk. You release it, wake it and watch it inflate until it ends up sad. As the story gets darker, the page does too, from white to grey to black.

## Step 3: Styleframe

The hero set the tone: red type on a white grid, with WILD in bold italic. The first styled version added W&LT’s own imagery, like the puk, collection photos and stickers.

## Step 4: Iteration

The first full design had a simple “start production” button for the too-big chapter. It became a mini game: drag the letters together to create W&LT before the timer runs out.

## Step 5: Build

Built with Vite, GSAP (ScrollTrigger, Draggable) and Lottie for the puk, and deployed on GitHub Pages.

Creating interactions that add to the story and are not meaningless.
